# Equal Eats' website, compared with ours

Read 2026-11-24 in a browser at 1280 and 390 pixels wide: the homepage, the
plastic and printable peanut card pages, Languages & Accuracy, About, FAQ,
Shipping, and Terms. Prices are what a US visitor saw that day.

## What they sell

- One card per allergen per language: English on one side, the chosen
  language on the other. 58 languages; allergies, celiac, intolerances,
  diets, custom cards.
- Plastic card $18.99 (ours: $18 placeholder). Printable PDF "from $9.99";
  the English printable is free. An app with a subscription.
- Bundle ladder on the product page: 2 cards −20%, 3 −25%, 5 −35%.
- The emergency card is a separate add-on ($7.99 printable, half price with
  a plastic card). Ours is in the set.
- The peanut card's wording is general ("Peanut can be found in: peanut
  butter, peanut oil…", "Does this food contain peanut?"). It is the same
  for every country that shares a language. Nothing about woks, shared oil
  or a mortar and pestle.

## How they earn trust

- Numbers in the hero and a strip under it: 4.9/5 from 2,008 reviews,
  50,000+ customers, 58 languages, 20 years, "3-step translation review".
  The peanut card page shows 1,144 reviews.
- The translation process, repeated on the homepage, the product page and
  its own page: professional translators, then proofreaders, then
  third-party native speakers. "Human process. No AI."
- Named team with photos, each with their own allergy story. Press logos.
- Photos of real people handing the card over. Live chat. A "card finder"
  quiz. Shipping, refund, privacy and terms pages. Currency picker.

## How they limit responsibility

- Languages & Accuracy page: "Cards help: communicate clearly, reduce
  misunderstandings. Cards don't replace: asking questions, medical advice,
  personal judgment."
- Terms (Allergy Translation Inc., Canada): "no guarantee whatsoever that
  the Products will fully communicate"; "in no way whatsoever liable for
  personal injury or death arising from a food allergy"; the buyer
  "represents and warrants" they have had medical advice and will take
  precautions beyond the card, such as carrying medicine; altered cards are
  at the buyer's risk. Much harder wording than our `/terms`.

## What it means for us

- Their site looks like a dependable, slightly generic Shopify store. Ours
  looks more designed. Theirs is more believable, because of the proof.
- Our real difference is the product, not the look: one allergy, written for
  how each country cooks, a yes-or-no question for the chef, and the
  emergency card and peanut guide included. Say that more plainly.
- Their loudest claim is a human translation process. Ours ("checked by a
  native speaker") is not yet true. This is the gap that matters most.
- Their card looks like an official notice: a bold headline, the
  crossed-out peanut, a few short lines. Ours is small dense text. The card
  redesign on the roadmap matters more than any page change.
- Worth copying when true: a short "how we check a card" section, a proof
  strip, real hand-over photos, shipping and refunds pages.
- Their terms name a company and disclaim injury outright. A lawyer will
  probably want ours closer to that.
